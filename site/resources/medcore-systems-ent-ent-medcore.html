1.1

ACUTE EPIGLOTTITIS

4-year-old child presents to emergency with acute-onset high fever (39.5°C), severe sore throat, muffled voice ('hot potato voice'), and difficulty breathing. Child is sitting upright, drooling, anxious, with chin thrust forward (tripod position). Parent reports onset over 4-6 hours. No cough. Vaccination status: declined routine immunizations. Examination: child distressed, stridor at rest, prefers upright position, refuses to lie flat. Oropharyngeal exam limited due to risk of airway compromise.

REVERSE-ENGINEERED PATTERN

How the exam tests it: Epiglottitis recognition from presentation (50%) + Airway management priority over all else (30%) + Thumb sign on X-ray (10%) + Hib vaccine relevance (10%)

Disguise used: 'Child with drooling and stridor', 'Tripod positioning', 'Hot potato voice', 'Rapid onset in unvaccinated child' (classic presentation — tests recognition of a pediatric airway emergency)

Discrimination tested: Epiglottitis = supraglottic (above vocal cords) vs Croup = subglottic (below). Epiglottitis = drooling + tripod + muffled voice + NO cough. Croup = barking cough + hoarse voice + stridor + cough. Airway management is PRIORITY #1 — do NOT examine oropharynx (can precipitate complete airway obstruction).

Common wrong answers: (1) Attempting oropharyngeal examination (can trigger laryngospasm and complete obstruction — DANGEROUS), (2) Starting antibiotics before securing airway (airway FIRST, antibiotics second), (3) Confusing with croup and ordering racemic epinephrine (wrong disease, wrong treatment), (4) Ordering lateral neck X-ray in unstable patient (send for imaging only if stable enough), (5) Missing that Hib vaccine has dramatically reduced incidence but cases still occur in unvaccinated

RECOGNITION TRIGGER

EMERGENCY TRIAD: (1) Acute onset (<12 hours) high fever + (2) Drooling + tripod positioning + (3) Stridor with muffled voice = Epiglottitis until proven otherwise — PRIORITY: SECURE AIRWAY

  • Age: Peak 2-6 years (but can occur in adults)
  • NO cough (vs croup which has barking cough)
  • Rapid onset (hours) vs croup (gradual over days)
  • Thumb sign on lateral neck X-ray (swollen epiglottis) — ONLY if stable enough for imaging

BEST INITIAL vs MOST ACCURATE

  • Best Initial Step: Assess airway stability and call anesthesia/ENT for emergent intubation
  • Most Accurate Diagnosis: Direct laryngoscopy under controlled setting shows cherry-red, swollen epiglottis
  • Most Accurate Imaging (if stable): Lateral neck X-ray shows thumb sign

IF-THEN: EMERGENCY AIRWAY MANAGEMENT

CHILD PRESENTS WITH SUSPECTED EPIGLOTTITIS:
IF airway compromised (stridor at rest, tripod, drooling, distressed)
→ THEN: DO NOT AGITATE — allow child to stay in parent's lap, no lying flat
+ Call anesthesia/ENT for emergent intubation in operating room
+ Have tracheostomy set ready (failed intubation backup)
+ Do NOT examine oropharynx, do NOT take blood, do NOT lie flat
IF stable and diagnosis uncertain
→ THEN: Portable lateral neck X-ray (upright, don't force position)
+ Thumb sign confirms diagnosis
+ Still prepare for intubation — condition can deteriorate rapidly
AFTER AIRWAY IS SECURED:
→ THEN: IV antibiotics (ceftriaxone or cefuroxime) × 7-10 days
+ Blood cultures
+ Hib vaccination status check and complete if needed

STAT vs CAN WAIT

STAT (ABSOLUTE AIRWAY EMERGENCY):

  • Call anesthesia/ENT for emergent intubation NOW
  • Take to OR for controlled intubation — do NOT attempt in ER/ward
  • Prepare tracheostomy set: nasal intubation may fail due to swollen epiglottis
  • Allow child to sit upright in parent's lap — do NOT force supine position

EXAM TRAPS

  • NEVER examine oropharynx with tongue blade — can trigger laryngospasm and complete obstruction
  • NEVER force child to lie flat — worsens airway obstruction (allow tripod position)
  • NEVER send for X-ray if airway unstable — portable X-ray only, upright position
  • Do NOT confuse with croup — croup has barking cough and steeple sign; epiglottitis has thumb sign
  • Do NOT forget: Hib vaccine has reduced incidence but unvaccinated children still at risk
  • Airway FIRST, antibiotics SECOND — never the reverse
  • In adults: epiglottitis is less common but more indolent — presents with severe sore throat out of proportion to exam
  • Haemophilus influenzae type b (Hib) is most common cause in unvaccinated; in adults: Strep pneumoniae, H. parainfluenzae
  • Nasotracheal intubation preferred (tube above swollen epiglottis) over orotracheal
  • Dexamethasone often given to reduce edema after airway secured

FOREIGN BODY AIRWAY OBSTRUCTION

18-month-old toddler found by mother with sudden-onset coughing, choking, and difficulty breathing while eating peanuts. Child now has wheezing, audible stridor, and intercostal retractions. No fever. No preceding illness. Mother reports the child was laughing while eating. Examination: distressed, cyanotic, stridor with respiratory distress. Oxygen saturation 88% on room air. Breath sounds decreased on right side with expiratory wheeze.

REVERSE-ENGINEERED PATTERN

How the exam tests it: Recognition of foreign body aspiration in children (40%) + Choking protocol (Heimlich maneuver) (30%) + Bronchoscopy as definitive management (20%) + Organic vs inorganic foreign body (10%)

Disguise used: 'Toddler with sudden choking while eating', 'Unilateral wheeze in a child', 'History of playing with small objects then coughing' (tests recognition of aspiration event)

Discrimination tested: Right main bronchus = most common aspiration site (wider, more vertical). Organic FB (peanut) causes more inflammation. Complete obstruction = Heimlich. Partial obstruction with good air exchange = encourage cough. Bronchoscopy = definitive diagnosis AND treatment.

Common wrong answers: (1) Blind finger sweep in mouth (can push FB further down — only use if visible), (2) Ordering chest X-ray and waiting for results in complete obstruction (airway first), (3) Missing that normal X-ray does NOT rule out FB aspiration (10-20% radiolucent), (4) Giving nebulized bronchodilators for unilateral wheeze (treating asthma when it's FB), (5) Not recognizing that delay causes granulation tissue and chronic complications

RECOGNITION TRIGGER

CLASSIC TRIAD: (1) Sudden onset choking/coughing while eating/playing + (2) Unilateral decreased breath sounds + (3) Expiratory wheeze = Foreign body aspiration until proven otherwise

  • Peak age: 1-3 years (exploratory mouthing behavior)
  • Most common FB: Peanuts, seeds, popcorn, small toys
  • Right main bronchus = most common site (60-70%)
  • Three phases: Initial choking → Asymptomatic period (days-weeks) → Complications (pneumonia, atelectasis, bronchiectasis)

BEST INITIAL vs MOST ACCURATE

  • Best Initial (complete obstruction in conscious child): Heimlich maneuver/abdominal thrusts
  • Best Initial (unconscious with complete obstruction): CPR with chest compressions, look for FB before each breath
  • Most Accurate Diagnostic Test: Rigid bronchoscopy (diagnostic + therapeutic)
  • Most Accurate Imaging: Inspiratory-expiratory chest X-ray or lateral decubitus (shows hyperinflation on affected side due to ball-valve effect)

IF-THEN: AIRWAY OBSTRUCTION MANAGEMENT

PATIENT WITH SUDDEN CHOKING:
IF Child <1 year old
→ THEN: Back blows (5x) + Chest thrusts (5x) alternating
IF Child ≥1 year or adult, conscious, complete obstruction
→ THEN: Heimlich maneuver (abdominal thrusts) — stand behind, fist above umbilicus, thrust inward and upward
IF Conscious but partial obstruction with good air exchange
→ THEN: Encourage forceful coughing — do NOT interfere
IF Unconscious
→ THEN: Start CPR — with each airway check, look for visible FB and remove if seen
AFTER AIRWAY CLEARED OR STABLE PARTIAL OBSTRUCTION:
→ THEN: Rigid bronchoscopy under general anesthesia to remove FB
+ Chest X-ray first if stable (inspiratory-expiratory)
+ IV antibiotics if infection present
+ Observe for post-obstructive pneumonia

STAT vs CAN WAIT

STAT (Complete obstruction or severe respiratory distress):

  • Immediate Heimlich maneuver / back blows / chest thrusts
  • Call 112/emergency services
  • If unconscious → CPR

CAN WAIT (Partial obstruction, stable):

  • Rigid bronchoscopy — can be scheduled semi-electively (same day)
  • Chest X-ray before procedure

EXAM TRAPS

  • NEVER do blind finger sweep — pushes FB deeper. Remove only if visible in mouth
  • NEVER give abdominal thrusts to infant <1 year — causes liver/spleen injury. Use back blows + chest thrusts
  • DO NOT confuse with asthma — unilateral wheeze + sudden onset = FB until proven otherwise
  • Normal chest X-ray does NOT rule out FB — 10-20% of FBs are radiolucent (peanuts, plastic)
  • Right main bronchus = most common site (wider angle, more vertical)
  • Organic FBs (peanuts, seeds) cause more inflammation and granulation tissue than inorganic (coins, toys)
  • Three-phase presentation: choking → asymptomatic interval (days) → pneumonia/atelectasis (don't miss the history!)
  • Expiratory X-ray shows hyperinflation on affected side (ball-valve effect — air enters but can't exit)
  • Rigid bronchoscopy = definitive management (NOT flexible bronchoscopy)
  • Complications: post-obstructive pneumonia, bronchiectasis, lung abscess, granulation tissue

EPISTAXIS

A 68-year-old man with hypertension presents to emergency with profuse right-sided nosebleed that started spontaneously 45 minutes ago. He reports a history of similar episodes, worse in winter. He takes aspirin 75 mg daily and amlodipine. He has tried pinching his nose and leaning forward at home but bleeding continues. On examination: BP 175/95, HR 95, actively bleeding from right nostril. Blood is seen trickling down the posterior pharynx. Anterior rhinoscopy shows no visible bleeding point. Patient appears anxious.

REVERSE-ENGINEERED PATTERN

How the exam tests it: First aid management (lean forward, pinch) (35%) + Anterior vs posterior epistaxis differentiation (30%) + Cauterization vs packing (20%) + Underlying causes (hypertension, coagulopathy) (15%)

Disguise used: 'Elderly hypertensive with nosebleed', 'Recurrent epistaxis in winter', 'Bleeding despite nasal pinch' (tests first aid knowledge and when to escalate)

Discrimination tested: Sit up + Lean forward + Pinch nostrils for 10-15 minutes = FIRST-LINE. Anterior epistaxis (90%) = Little's area (Kiesselbach plexus) → silver nitrate cautery. Posterior epistaxis (10%) = sphenopalatine artery → posterior packing or balloon catheter. Blood in posterior pharynx = posterior bleed until proven otherwise.

Common wrong answers: (1) Tilting head BACK (causes blood to trickle down throat → nausea/vomiting/aspiration — LEAN FORWARD always), (2) Packing nose immediately without trying direct pressure first, (3) Missing posterior epistaxis when anterior exam is clear (think posterior if blood in oropharynx), (4) Not checking for underlying coagulopathy or anticoagulant use, (5) Silver nitrate cautery contraindicated if bilateral (risk of septal perforation)

RECOGNITION TRIGGER

KEY POINTS:

  • Little's area (Kiesselbach plexus) = most common bleeding site (anterior septum)
  • 90% anterior, 10% posterior
  • Blood in posterior pharynx = posterior bleed or heavy anterior bleed
  • Worse in winter (dry air → nasal mucosa drying → crusting → epistaxis)

BEST INITIAL vs MOST ACCURATE

  • Best Initial: Sit upright + Lean forward + Pinch nostrils for 10-15 minutes continuously
  • Most Accurate (anterior): Anterior rhinoscopy with headlight — identify bleeding point → silver nitrate cautery
  • Most Accurate (posterior): Nasal endoscopy by ENT specialist

IF-THEN: EPISTAXIS MANAGEMENT

PATIENT WITH ACTIVE NOSEBLEED:
IF First-line measures (sit up, lean forward, pinch × 10-15 min) → bleeding stops
→ THEN: Reassure, avoid nose blowing/trauma for 24h, apply petroleum jelly to nasal mucosa
IF Bleeding continues despite first-line measures
→ THEN: Anterior rhinoscopy
+ Visible bleeding point → Silver nitrate cautery (unilateral only)
+ No visible point / bilateral bleeding → Anterior packing (Merocel, ribbon gauze)
IF Anterior packing fails OR posterior pharynx blood seen
→ THEN: Posterior packing (Foley balloon catheter) OR Epistaxis balloon
+ ENT consultation
+ Consider: Chemical cautery if point visible, electrocautery, arterial ligation (sphenopalatine/ethmoid) if refractory
IF Hemodynamically unstable or ongoing bleeding despite packing
→ THEN: Resuscitate + Blood transfusion + ENT emergently for arterial ligation/embolization

STAT vs CAN WAIT

STAT (Hemodynamic instability, ongoing heavy bleeding, posterior bleed):

  • IV access, cross-match, resuscitation
  • ENT consult urgently
  • Consider nasal balloon or posterior pack

CAN WAIT (Bleeding controlled, stable patient):

  • Observation, blood pressure management
  • Identify and treat underlying cause

EXAM TRAPS

  • NEVER tilt head back — LEAN FORWARD to prevent blood from going down throat
  • Pinch continuously for 10-15 minutes — releasing to 'check' disrupts clot formation
  • Silver nitrate cautery only on ONE side of septum — bilateral cautery risks septal perforation
  • Posterior epistaxis = harder to control, higher morbidity in elderly, needs ENT
  • Hypertension can both cause and worsen epistaxis — manage BP
  • Anticoagulants/antiplatelets increase severity and recurrence — don't stop without consulting prescriber
  • HHT (Hereditary Hemorrhagic Telangiectasia / Osler-Weber-Rendu) = recurrent epistaxis + telangiectasias → suspect if recurrent family history
  • Nasal packing complications: hypoxia (nasopulmonary reflex), toxic shock syndrome (leave pack <48h), septal hematoma/abscess
  • Septal hematoma (post-trauma) can cause avascular necrosis of nasal cartilage — needs urgent drainage
  • In children: epistaxis is usually anterior and self-limiting — topical anesthetic + vasoconstrictor + silver nitrate works well

ACUTE OTITIS MEDIA (AOM) vs OTITIS MEDIA WITH EFFUSION (OME)

A 2-year-old boy presents with 3-day history of fever (39°C), irritability, crying, and tugging at his left ear. He has had runny nose and cough for 5 days. No vomiting. Mother reports he has been waking up at night crying. On examination: Left tympanic membrane is bulging, opaque, erythematous with decreased mobility on pneumatic otoscopy. Right ear normal.

REVERSE-ENGINEERED PATTERN

How the exam tests it: AOM vs OME differentiation (40%) + Otoscopic findings (30%) + Antibiotic decision (20%) + Tympanostomy tubes indication (10%)

Disguise used: 'Child with ear pain and fever', 'Irritable child tugging at ear', 'Failed hearing test at school' (tests ability to differentiate AOM from OME from otitis externa)

Discrimination tested: AOM = acute onset + fever + ear pain + bulging/opaque TM + erythema + IMMOBILITY on pneumatic otoscopy. OME = effusion behind TM WITHOUT acute symptoms (no fever, no pain). Key: AOM needs antibiotics (amoxicillin first-line), OME does NOT (watchful waiting 3 months).

Common wrong answers: (1) Treating OME with antibiotics (no infection = no benefit), (2) Not diagnosing AOM because TM is not erythematous (erythema alone is NOT diagnostic — must have bulging AND immobility), (3) Prescribing amoxicillin for ALL otitis media (OME doesn't need it), (4) Referring for tympanostomy tubes for first episode of OME (indicated only if effusion persists >3 months with hearing loss), (5) Missing mastoiditis complication (post-auricular swelling, protruding pinna)

RECOGNITION TRIGGER

AOM TRIAD: (1) Acute onset (<48h) + (2) Middle ear effusion (bulging/immobile TM) + (3) Inflammation (otalgia, fever, erythema)

OME: Middle ear effusion WITHOUT signs/symptoms of acute infection — TM may be retracted or have air-fluid level, mobile or immobile, but NOT bulging/erythematous

BEST INITIAL vs MOST ACCURATE

  • Best Initial Test: Pneumatic otoscopy (diagnostic — assesses TM mobility)
  • Best Initial Treatment (AOM): Amoxicillin 80-90 mg/kg/day × 10 days (or single-dose ceftriaxone if severe)
  • Most Accurate (OME): Tympanometry (shows flat curve Type B — confirms effusion) + Audiometry (assesses hearing loss)

IF-THEN: MANAGEMENT

CHILD PRESENTS WITH EAR SYMPTOMS:
IF AOM diagnosed (acute onset + bulging/immobile TM + fever/pain)
→ THEN: Amoxicillin × 10 days (first-line)
+ Analgesics (acetaminophen/ibuprofen)
+ If amoxicillin failure after 48-72h → Amoxicillin-clavulanate
+ If severe pain/toxicity → Tympanocentesis (+ culture)
IF OME diagnosed (effusion without acute symptoms)
→ THEN: Watchful waiting × 3 months (90% resolve spontaneously)
+ Audiometry — if hearing loss >20 dB, shorter observation
+ No antibiotics, no decongestants, no steroids (no benefit)
IF OME persists >3 months WITH hearing loss
→ THEN: Tympanostomy tube placement (ventilation tubes)
+ Adenoidectomy if adenoid hypertrophy present
IF Complications suspected (mastoiditis, intracranial extension)
→ THEN: IV antibiotics + ENT consult + CT temporal bone

STAT vs CAN WAIT

STAT (Complicated AOM):

  • Mastoiditis (post-auricular swelling, erythema, protruding pinna)
  • Intracranial extension (meningitis, brain abscess, sigmoid sinus thrombosis)
  • Facial nerve paralysis
  • Labyrinthitis (vertigo, nystagmus, sensorineural hearing loss)

CAN WAIT (Uncomplicated AOM or OME):

  • AOM → Oral antibiotics as outpatient
  • OME → Scheduled follow-up in 3 months

EXAM TRAPS

  • Erythema of TM alone is NOT diagnostic of AOM — must have bulging AND immobility (erythema can occur from crying/fever)
  • Do NOT treat OME with antibiotics — it's effusion without infection, antibiotics provide no benefit
  • Amoxicillin is first-line for AOM — NOT amoxicillin-clavulanate (reserve for treatment failure)
  • AOM in children <6 months = more aggressive treatment (may need ceftriaxone or ENT referral)
  • Recurrent AOM (≥3 episodes in 6 months or ≥4 in 12 months) → consider tympanostomy tubes
  • Mastoiditis: post-auricular swelling + protruding pinna + fever → CT temporal bone + IV antibiotics + mastoidectomy
  • Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae, Moraxella catarrhalis = most common AOM pathogens
  • Breastfeeding reduces AOM risk (protective effect)
  • Risk factors: daycare attendance, pacifier use, bottle-feeding supine, smoke exposure
  • OME can cause speech/language delay if bilateral hearing loss persists — monitor carefully

CHRONIC SUPPURATIVE OTITIS MEDIA (CSOM) & CHOLESTEATOMA

A 35-year-old woman presents with 6-month history of persistent left ear discharge (purulent, foul-smelling) and progressive hearing loss. She reports intermittent ear pain and a feeling of fullness. No fever. She had multiple episodes of ear infections as a child. On examination: Left ear — purulent discharge in ear canal, TM perforation seen, pearly white mass visible through perforation in the attic region. Right ear normal. Audiometry shows moderate conductive hearing loss on left.

REVERSE-ENGINEERED PATTERN

How the exam tests it: CSOM with cholesteatoma recognition (35%) + Safe vs unsafe perforation (30%) + Cholesteatoma complications (20%) + Medical vs surgical management (15%)

Disguise used: 'Long-standing ear discharge with hearing loss', 'Foul-smelling otorrhea', 'Pearly white mass behind TM' (tests recognition of cholesteatoma as surgical disease)

Discrimination tested: CSOM = chronic inflammation of middle ear + mastoid with TM perforation + discharge >6 weeks. Cholesteatoma = keratinizing squamous epithelium in middle ear → erosive, destructive. SAFE (tubotympanic) = central perforation, resolves with aural toilet + abx drops. UNSAFE (atticoantral) = attic perforation, associated with cholesteatoma, SURGICAL — does NOT resolve with medical therapy alone.

Common wrong answers: (1) Treating cholesteatoma with antibiotics alone (needs surgical removal — medical therapy only controls infection), (2) Confusing safe (central perforation) with unsafe (attic perforation) CSOM, (3) Missing intracranial complications of cholesteatoma (meningitis, brain abscess, sigmoid sinus thrombosis), (4) Not recognizing that cholesteatoma causes ossicular erosion and permanent hearing loss if untreated, (5) Prescribing ototoxic ear drops (aminoglycosides) if TM perforation present

RECOGNITION TRIGGER

CSOM TRIAD: (1) Chronic otorrhea (>6 weeks) + (2) TM perforation + (3) Conductive hearing loss

Cholesteatoma: Pearly white mass in attic/middle ear + Foul-smelling discharge + Marginal/attic perforation = UNSAFE ear → SURGERY

  • Best Initial: Otoscopic examination under microscope (identify perforation site, cholesteatoma)
  • Most Accurate Imaging: Non-contrast HRCT temporal bone (assesses extent of disease, ossicular erosion, intracranial extension)
  • Most Accurate Diagnosis: Surgical exploration (confirmatory + therapeutic)

IF-THEN: CSOM MANAGEMENT

PATIENT WITH CHRONIC EAR DISCHARGE >6 WEEKS:
IF Safe CSOM (central perforation, no cholesteatoma)
→ THEN: Aural toilet (microsuction cleaning)
+ Topical antibiotic drops (Ciprofloxacin/dexamethasone) × 2 weeks
+ Avoid ototoxic drops (neomycin, gentamicin) if TM perforation
+ If dry after 2 weeks → Observe, consider tympanoplasty if perforation persists
IF Unsafe CSOM (attic perforation, suspect cholesteatoma)
→ THEN: HRCT temporal bone to assess extent
+ ENT referral — SURGERY indicated
+ Procedure: Mastoidectomy (remove cholesteatoma) ± Tympanoplasty (repair TM)
+ Surgery is curative — medical therapy alone insufficient (does not remove cholesteatoma matrix)
IF Complications present (facial palsy, vertigo, meningitis, brain abscess)
→ THEN: Urgent CT/MRI + IV antibiotics + ENT + Neurosurgery
+ Immediate surgical exploration

EXAM TRAPS

  • Cholesteatoma = SURGICAL disease — antibiotics alone do NOT cure it (only control superinfection)
  • NEVER prescribe ototoxic ear drops (gentamicin, neomycin) if TM perforation present — can cause sensorineural hearing loss. Ciprofloxacin drops are safe.
  • Safe = central perforation (pars tensa), responds to medical therapy. Unsafe = attic/marginal perforation (pars flaccida), needs surgery.
  • Cholesteatoma is NOT cancer — it's keratinizing squamous epithelium that behaves like a benign tumor (locally destructive)
  • Complications: Erosion of ossicles (hearing loss), facial nerve canal (facial palsy), tegmen tympani (meningitis, brain abscess), lateral semicircular canal (fistula → vertigo), sigmoid sinus (thrombosis)
  • HRCT temporal bone = imaging of choice for cholesteatoma (NOT MRI unless intracranial complication suspected)

A 60-year-old factory worker presents with progressive hearing loss in both ears over several years. Disguise used: 'Elderly patient with hearing loss', 'Factory worker with tinnitus', 'Child with recurrent ear infections and hearing loss' (tests tuning fork and audiometry interpretation)

Skull base invasion is the defining feature of advanced disease and the determinant of resectability.

Discriminator: Cranial nerve and orbital signs = skull base invasion = advanced, not a benign pressure effect.

A) Benign polyp pressing on the hard palateA benign polyp does not cause cranial nerve V2 anesthesia or diplopia
B) Dental caries referred symptomDental pain is local - it does not produce cheek numbness and diplopia together
D) Trigeminal neuralgiaTrigeminal neuralgia is lancinating episodic pain, not constant numbness with diplopia
E) Allergic rhinitis with eustachian dysfunctionAllergic rhinitis does not invade the skull base or orbit

Trap: Underestimate trap - the benign mechanisms sound plausible; nerve/eye signs are structurally incompatible with them.

Future alert: Cheek numbness (CN V2) ± diplopia in sinonasal mass = skull base invasion - advanced, resectability questioned.

Q45 : A 60-year-old man who worked in a textile mill for 30 years presents with progressive bilateral hearing loss and roaring tinnitus. He has no ear pain or discharge. Audiometry shows bilateral high-frequency sensorineural loss with a 4 kHz notch. Tympanic membranes are normal. What is the most likely diagnosis?
  • A) Noise-induced hearing loss
  • B) Presbycusis
  • C) Otosclerosis
  • D) Meniere disease
  • E) Acoustic neuroma

Correct: A) Noise-induced hearing loss

Concept: Noise-induced hearing loss - 4 kHz notch + occupational noise Interpretation

Why A: Bilateral high-frequency sensorineural loss with a classic 4 kHz notch plus 30 years of occupational noise is noise-induced hearing loss (NIHL). It is irreversible and typically begins at 4 kHz, spreading with continued exposure. The occupational history - not age alone - is the discriminator against presbycusis.

Discriminator: 4 kHz notch + noise exposure = NIHL; presbycusis is the 'aging' diagnosis without the exposure history.

B) PresbycusisPresbycusis is age-related bilateral high-frequency loss - it does not require or explain the mill history or notch
C) OtosclerosisOtosclerosis is a CONDUCTIVE loss with a normal TM in young adults - not bilateral SNHL with tinnitus in a mill worker
D) Meniere diseaseMeniere has episodic vertigo with fluctuating hearing - no noise exposure, no 4 kHz notch pattern
E) Acoustic neuromaAcoustic neuroma is UNILATERAL progressive SNHL with tinnitus - this is bilateral and occupational

Q46 : A 45-year-old man has a conductive hearing loss in the LEFT ear from otitis media with effusion. A 512 Hz tuning fork Weber test is performed. Where does the sound lateralize?
  • A) To the RIGHT (normal) ear
  • B) To the LEFT (affected) ear
  • C) It remains midline
  • D) It cannot be assessed with a conductive loss
  • E) It alternates between ears

Correct: B) To the LEFT (affected) ear

Concept: Weber test - lateralizes to the conductive-loss ear Recall

Why B: In a unilateral CONDUCTIVE hearing loss, the Weber test lateralizes to the AFFECTED (poorer) ear - the bone-conducted sound is heard louder on that side because ambient noise is excluded by the conductive block. In a sensORINEURAL loss it lateralizes to the BETTER (normal) ear. The lateralization direction is the entire point of the test.

Discriminator: Conductive → louder in the BAD ear; sensorineural → louder in the GOOD ear. Learn the flip once.

A) To the RIGHT (normal) earLateralization to the normal ear is the sensORINEURAL pattern - the conductive loss localizes to the affected side
C) It remains midlineMidline Weber means symmetric hearing - a unilateral conductive loss forces a lateralizing sound
D) It cannot be assessed with a conductive lossThe Weber test is precisely designed for unilateral losses - it is fully assessable
E) It alternates between earsAlternation is not a Weber finding - the pattern is fixed by the loss type

Trap: Direction trap - intuitive physics says 'quiet ear hears less', but the conductive block makes that ear hear bone conduction louder.

Future alert: Weber: conductive loss → lateralizes to affected ear; sensorineural loss → lateralizes to the normal ear.

Q47 : A 52-year-old woman woke this morning with a sudden feeling of a blocked left ear and decreased hearing, present for 8 hours. She has no pain or discharge; otoscopy shows normal tympanic membranes. What is the most appropriate management?
  • A) Reassure and recheck in 6 months
  • B) Amoxicillin for 10 days for presumed otitis media
  • C) Urgent audiometry, and if sudden sensorineural hearing loss is confirmed, high-dose oral prednisolone (60 mg daily) started within 72 hours
  • D) Myringotomy with tympanostomy tube placement
  • E) Vestibular suppressants only

Correct: C) Urgent audiometry, and if sudden sensorineural hearing loss is confirmed, high-dose oral prednisolone (60 mg daily) started within 72 hours

Concept: Sudden sensorineural hearing loss - steroid window within 72 hours Interpretation

Why C: Sudden-onset unilateral hearing loss with a normal TM is sudden sensorineural hearing loss (SSNHL), defined as >30 dB loss in 3 contiguous frequencies within 72 hours. The treatment standard is high-dose oral prednisolone (60 mg daily, tapered) started within 72 hours of onset; spontaneous recovery occurs in about 50%, and early steroids improve outcomes. This is an otologic emergency - 6-month rechecks and antibiotics are wrong.

Discriminator: Sudden hearing loss = audiometry now + prednisolone within 72 h; the normal TM rules out otitis media.

A) Reassure and recheck in 6 monthsSSNHL is time-critical - reassuring away 6 months forfeits the treatment window
B) Amoxicillin for 10 days for presumed otitis mediaAmoxicillin treats otitis media - the TM is normal and there is no infection
D) Myringotomy with tympanostomy tube placementMyringotomy treats effusion, not SSNHL - the middle ear is normal
E) Vestibular suppressants onlyVestibular suppressants treat vertigo, not sudden hearing loss

Trap: Banal-presentation trap - 'blocked ear, no pain' is made to look trivial; the 72-hour clock makes it an emergency.

Future alert: SSNHL: audiometry + prednisolone 60 mg within 72 hours; ~50% spontaneous recovery; act on the clock.

Future alert: Unilateral SNHL + tinnitus = acoustic neuroma screening; MRI internal auditory meatus with gadolinium.

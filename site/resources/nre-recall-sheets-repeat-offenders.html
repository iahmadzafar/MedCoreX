Cytomegalovirus (CMV)

The transplant-window virus. If the vignette mentions an immunocompromised host and an organ, CMV is whispering from the options.

peak reactivation window post-transplant
CD4 < 50/mm³
the AIDS threshold below which it strikes
> 10,000 copies/mL
viral load favouring reactivation in HIV
asymptomatic
most common presentation in adults

Key points

  • Most common in adults, usually asymptomatic.
  • Reactivation occurs in immunocompromised patients (e.g. transplant, HIV).
  • Transmission: sexual contact, vertical (congenital), breast milk, respiratory droplets, blood transfusion.
  • Risk of reactivation: organ transplant (first 100 days) and HIV with CD4 < 50/mm³ or viral load > 10,000 copies/mL.

Hallmarks

  • Most often asymptomatic.
  • Reactivation in immunocompromised.
  • In transplant: within first 100 days.
  • In HIV: CD4 < 50/mm³ or viral load > 10,000 copies/mL.

Clinical features — where it lands

  • Retinitis — the AIDS / CD4 < 50 eye.
  • GI & hepatobiliary — colitis + hepatitis.
  • Esophagitis — odynophagia.
  • Pneumonitis — transplant & hematologic.
  • CNS — the diffuse patterns.

Clinical features

  • CMV retinitis — seen in AIDS (CD4 < 50/mm³).
  • Painless visual loss.
  • Floating spots (floaters).
  • Retinal detachment.
  • “Pizza pie” appearance — white, fluffy, perivascular lesions.
  • GI & hepatobiliary: nonspecific symptoms, bloody diarrhea, abdominal pain, hepatitis / cholangitis — can contribute to AIDS cholangiopathy.
  • CMV esophagitis: odynophagia (painful swallowing), shallow distal esophageal ulcers; seen in immunocompromised (CD4 < 50/mm³).
  • Pneumonitis: fever, cough, dyspnea, sparse sputum; high mortality; more common in hematologic malignancies and transplant patients than in AIDS.

CNS involvement

  • Polyradiculopathy
  • Transverse myelitis
  • Subacute encephalitis
  • Periventricular calcifications
  • Seen in immunocompromised (CD4 < 50/mm³).

Diagnosis

  • Virus isolation / culture.
  • PCR — serum or tissue.
  • Histopathology — gold standard for tissue-invasive disease.

Treatment

  • Ganciclovir — first line.
  • Valganciclovir — oral.
  • Foscarnet — if resistant.
  • Treat underlying disease / reduce immunosuppression if possible.

Exam eye

  • Transplant + first 100 days → think CMV.
  • AIDS + painless visual loss → retinitis.
  • Odynophagia + shallow distal ulcers in an immunocompromised host → esophagitis.

Gonorrhea

Discharge + dysuria + a partner who also needs treating. The intracellular diplococcus with an IV rule for disseminated disease.

Key points

  • Caused by a gram negative intracellular diplococcus.
  • Can infect almost any site in the female reproductive tract.
  • In males infection is usually limited to the urethra.

Clinical features

  • In females: greenish-yellow discharge, pelvic or adnexal pain and swollen Bartholin glands.
  • In males: purulent urethral discharge, dysuria and erythema of urethral meatus.

Diagnosis

  • Gold standard: Gram stain and culture from relevant site (pharynx, cervix, urethra or anus).
  • Nucleic acid amplification tests can be done on urine or genital samples.
  • In disseminated infection: may present with monoarticular septic arthritis, rash and/or tenosynovitis.

Treatment

  • Intramuscular ceftriaxone.
  • Oral azithromycin (regardless of co-infection).
  • Condoms are effective for prevention.
  • Sexual partner(s) should be treated if possible.
  • Fluoroquinolones should not be used due to resistance.
  • In disseminated infection: IV ceftriaxone for at least 24 hours.

Differential diagnosis

Chlamydia infectionEndometriosisPharyngitisPelvic inflammatory diseaseVaginitisUrinary tract infectionSalpingitisTubo-ovarian abscess

Complications

  • Persistent infection with pain
  • Infertility
  • Tubo-ovarian abscess with rupture
  • Disseminated infection: migratory polyarthralgia, tenosynovitis and pustular skin lesions

Exam eye

  • IM ceftriaxone — the treatment reflex; fluoroquinolones are out due to resistance.
  • IV only when disease disseminates → for at least 24 hours.
  • Oral azithromycin regardless of co-infection, and sexual partner(s) treated if possible.

Types of UTI — criteria & treatment

Five presentations, one decision tree each: who qualifies, and what to prescribe. Pick a type and work the two columns.

criteria · acute cystitis

  • Lower UTI in an otherwise healthy, nonpregnant adult woman.
  • Dysuria, frequency, urgency, suprapubic pain.
  • Infection confined to the bladder.
  • No systemic symptoms.

treatment

  • Nitrofurantoin for 5 days.
  • TMP-SMX for 3 days (if local resistance low and appropriate).
  • Nitrofurantoin not suitable if suspected pyelonephritis or complicated UTI.
  • Urine culture if treatment fails or recurrence/atypical features.

criteria · higher-risk host

  • Does not meet criteria for uncomplicated UTI.
  • Higher risk setting: pregnancy · male sex · diabetes or other comorbidities · immunocompromised · urinary catheter or stent · urinary tract obstruction · recurrent or refractory UTI.
  • May present with fever, chills, vomiting, abdominal or flank pain (pyelonephritis).

treatment

  • Obtain urine culture.
  • Antibiotics according to severity, local resistance and culture results.
  • If hemodynamically unstable, septic, or unable to take oral treatment → urgent hospital assessment and IV antibiotics (e.g. third/fourth generation cephalosporin or as per local policy).

criteria · gestational

  • Routine screening for asymptomatic bacteriuria.
  • May present with acute cystitis.
  • Increased risk of pyelonephritis and urosepsis.

treatment

  • Asymptomatic bacteriuria should be treated.
  • Pregnancy-appropriate antibiotics guided by culture (e.g. nitrofurantoin or cephalexin/amoxicillin if susceptible).
  • Follow-up culture may be advised.
  • Pyelonephritis requires urgent assessment, often inpatient IV therapy.
  • Avoid antibiotics unsuitable for the trimester or suspected upper-tract infection.

criteria · definition & behaviors

  • Recurrent UTI = ≥ 2 infections in 6 months or ≥ 3 infections in 12 months.
  • Behavioral measures first line: adequate fluid intake · postcoital voiding · avoid spermicides · vaginal estrogen in postmenopausal women (when appropriate).

treatment / prevention

  • If behavioral measures are ineffective:
  • Low-dose antibiotic prophylaxis (postcoital or continuous) after evaluation and discussion of risks/benefits.
  • Choice: TMP-SMX or nitrofurantoin (as per local resistance).

criteria · an infection look-alike

  • Chronic suprapubic pain or discomfort related to bladder.
  • Associated with frequency and urgency.
  • Symptoms > 6 weeks without an underlying infection or other identifiable cause.
  • May mimic UTI.

treatment

  • Avoid individual dietary triggers.
  • Symptom management (e.g. amitriptyline).
  • Bladder instillations or hydrodistension (selected cases).
  • Multimodal approach, often with specialist input.

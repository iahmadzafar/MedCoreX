Acute Angle-Closure Glaucoma

VIGNETTE PATTERN

Patient age: 60-70 years old (peak incidence)

Chief complaint: Sudden severe unilateral eye pain + headache + nausea/vomiting (often mistaken for migraine or GI pathology)

Vision changes: Blurred vision + halos around lights (pathognomonic for corneal edema)

Trigger history: Recent dark environment exposure (movie theater, dim restaurant) OR recent mydriatic drop use (dilated eye exam) OR anticholinergic medication (antihistamines, TCAs)

RECOGNITION TRIGGER (The Finding That Locks It)

The pathognomonic triad:

  • Mid-dilated pupil (4-6 mm) — NOT reactive to light (this is critical; reactive pupil rules out angle-closure)
  • Rock-hard eye on gentle palpation through closed lid
  • Conjunctival injection (diffuse redness)

Additional findings: Cloudy/hazy cornea (from edema), shallow anterior chamber on slit lamp, ciliary flush

Key discriminator: Pupil size is the fastest differentiator — mid-dilated = angle-closure; small = uveitis; normal = migraine

CONFIRMING TEST (What Seals the Diagnosis)

Tonometry: IOP >21 mmHg (often 40-80 mmHg) — this confirms elevated pressure

Slit lamp exam: Shallow anterior chamber (best seen with oblique lighting), corneal edema, mid-dilated pupil

Gonioscopy: Closed angle (gold standard, but NOT done acutely — done after IOP controlled)

IMMEDIATE MANAGEMENT (What to Do Now)

Goal: Lower IOP emergently to prevent permanent vision loss

Step 1 — Pharmacologic IOP reduction (do ALL of these):

  • Topical beta-blocker (timolol) — decreases aqueous production
  • Topical alpha-agonist (apraclonidine) — decreases production
  • Systemic carbonic anhydrase inhibitor (acetazolamide 500mg IV/PO) — decreases production
  • Hyperosmotic agent (mannitol 1-2 g/kg IV OR oral glycerol) — pulls fluid from eye

Step 2 — After IOP lowered:

  • Topical pilocarpine (miotic) — constricts pupil, opens angle (give AFTER IOP reduced; if given when IOP >40, paradoxically worsens closure)

Step 3 — Definitive treatment:

  • Laser peripheral iridotomy (LPI) — creates hole in iris to equalize pressure between chambers. Do in BOTH eyes (fellow eye has 50% risk)

EXAM TRAPS (Wrong Answers They Want You to Pick)

  • Giving pilocarpine immediately — correct drug, but IOP lowering comes first. If IOP >40, pilocarpine worsens ischemia and angle closure
  • Mistaking for migraine — nausea + headache overlap, but migraine has normal pupil and no halos
  • Dilating the pupil — NEVER give mydriatics (worsens closure)
  • Treating only the affected eye — contralateral eye needs prophylactic LPI (50% will develop attack)
  • Sending patient home with oral medications — this is an emergency requiring immediate ophthalmology consult

KEY NUMBERS

  • Normal IOP: 10-21 mmHg
  • Acute angle-closure IOP: typically 40-80 mmHg
  • Mid-dilated pupil: 4-6 mm (vs normal 3-4 mm in light, 6-8 mm in dark)
  • Fellow eye risk: 50% within 5 years without prophylactic treatment
  • Peak age: 60-70 years (hyperopes, Asians > Caucasians)

Anterior Uveitis

VIGNETTE PATTERN

Patient age: Young to middle-aged adult (20-50 years, peak 20-40)

Chief complaint: Deep, aching unilateral eye pain + photophobia (hallmark) + blurred vision

Pain character: Dull, boring pain that worsens with accommodation (reading, near work)

Associated history: HLA-B27-associated conditions — ankylosing spondylitis (young male with back stiffness), reactive arthritis (recent GI/GU infection), inflammatory bowel disease (Crohn's, UC), psoriatic arthritis. May also see in sarcoidosis, Behçet's, or idiopathic

RECOGNITION TRIGGER (The Finding That Locks It)

The diagnostic tetrad:

  • Ciliary flush (perilimbal injection) — redness concentrated around cornea, NOT diffuse conjunctival injection
  • Cells and flare in anterior chamber on slit lamp (inflammatory cells floating in aqueous humor, "headlights in fog" appearance)
  • Small (constricted) pupil — miosis from ciliary spasm (opposite of angle-closure)
  • Pain on accommodation — pathognomonic (pain when focusing on near objects)

Classic exam findings: Hypopyon (pus layering in anterior chamber, seen in severe cases or HLA-B27), posterior synechiae (iris stuck to lens from inflammation, causes irregular pupil), keratic precipitates (KP) on corneal endothelium

Memory aid: "Small pupil, deep pain" = uveitis. "Mid-dilated pupil, rock-hard eye" = angle-closure.

CONFIRMING TEST (What Seals the Diagnosis)

Slit lamp examination: Cells and flare in anterior chamber (diagnostic). Use high magnification with narrow beam.

Look for specific findings:

  • Hypopyon — layered pus in anterior chamber (severe uveitis, think HLA-B27 or Behçet's)
  • Posterior synechiae — iris adheres to lens (causes irregular, "scalloped" pupil)
  • Keratic precipitates (KP) — inflammatory cell deposits on corneal endothelium ("mutton-fat" if granulomatous)

SYSTEMIC WORKUP (When to Order)

First episode in young patient: Consider observation without extensive workup (50% idiopathic)

Recurrent or bilateral uveitis: Workup for systemic causes

  • HLA-B27 — if positive, screen for spondyloarthropathies
  • RPR/VDRL — rule out syphilis (always check in uveitis)
  • ANA, ACE, CXR — if suspect autoimmune (lupus, sarcoidosis)
  • Quantiferon or PPD — rule out TB (especially if granulomatous)

TREATMENT (How to Manage)

Goals: Reduce inflammation, prevent posterior synechiae, relieve pain

First-line therapy:

  • Topical corticosteroids (prednisolone acetate 1% every 1-2 hours initially) — mainstay of treatment
  • Cycloplegic agent (cyclopentolate or homatropine) — dilates pupil, relieves ciliary spasm (pain relief), prevents synechiae formation

Severe or refractory cases:

  • Systemic corticosteroids OR immunosuppressants (methotrexate, azathioprine) if recurrent/chronic

Monitoring: Check IOP during steroid treatment (steroids can ↑ IOP in steroid responders)

EXAM TRAPS (Wrong Answers They Want You to Pick)

  • Starting antibiotics empirically — this is inflammatory, not infectious (unless you see ulcer/infiltrate suggesting endophthalmitis)
  • Confusing with conjunctivitis — conjunctivitis has discharge, NO photophobia, NO cells/flare, NO pain with accommodation
  • Not giving cycloplegic — cycloplegics are essential (prevent synechiae AND relieve pain)
  • Missing systemic associations — always ask about back pain (ankylosing spondylitis), recent diarrhea (reactive arthritis), bowel symptoms (IBD)
  • Not checking for syphilis — RPR/VDRL is mandatory in uveitis workup ("the great masquerader")

KEY NUMBERS

  • Peak age: 20-40 years (younger than angle-closure)
  • HLA-B27 prevalence: 50% in acute anterior uveitis (vs 8% in general population)
  • Recurrence rate: 50% within 10 years (higher if HLA-B27+)
  • Idiopathic cases: ~50% (no systemic cause found)

Herpetic Keratitis

VIGNETTE PATTERN

Chief complaint: Unilateral eye pain + foreign body sensation + photophobia + tearing + blurred vision

Key history clues:

  • Contact lens wearer (overlap with bacterial keratitis, especially Pseudomonas)
  • Immunocompromised (HIV, transplant, chemotherapy)
  • History of cold sores or previous herpetic eye infection (HSV recurs)
  • Recent fever or URI (may trigger reactivation)

Pain severity: Moderate to severe (worse than simple conjunctivitis, similar to bacterial ulcer)

RECOGNITION TRIGGER (The Pathognomonic Finding)

The diagnosis-locking finding:

Dendritic (branching) ulcer on fluorescein staining — tree-like branching pattern with terminal bulbs ("rose thorns"). This is PATHOGNOMONIC for HSV keratitis.

Additional supportive findings:

  • Decreased corneal sensation (test with cotton wisp — affected eye has reduced/absent sensation compared to normal eye)
  • Vesicular rash on eyelid or periorbital area (if herpes zoster ophthalmicus — see Hutchinson sign)
  • Sectoral iris atrophy (chronic cases with recurrent inflammation)

Contact lens wearer + round ulcer: Think Pseudomonas (needs fortified antibiotics: tobramycin + ceftazidime)

Contact lens wearer + dendritic ulcer: Still HSV (contact lens history is a red herring)

CONFIRMING TEST (What Seals the Diagnosis)

Fluorescein staining: Dendritic ulcer pattern is diagnostic (no further testing needed for typical cases)

Corneal sensation testing: Decreased sensation supports HSV (use cotton wisp, compare to unaffected eye)

If atypical or uncertain:

  • PCR or viral culture from corneal scraping (gold standard, rarely needed)
  • Immunofluorescence (direct fluorescent antibody for HSV antigen)

TREATMENT (The Non-Negotiables)

First-line therapy:

  • Topical antivirals:
  • – Ganciclovir 0.15% gel 5x/day OR
  • – Trifluridine 1% drops 9x/day (more frequent dosing)

Severe or stromal involvement:

  • Oral acyclovir 400mg 5x/day OR valacyclovir 500mg TID (better bioavailability)

If stromal keratitis (immune-mediated):

  • Topical corticosteroids ONLY after epithelium heals AND with concurrent antiviral coverage (steroids alone worsen epithelial HSV)

ABSOLUTE CONTRAINDICATIONS

NEVER give topical steroids in active epithelial HSV keratitis

  • Steroids suppress immune response → dendritic ulcer progresses to
  • "geographic ulcer" (large, map-like, amoeboid ulcer) → permanent scarring and vision loss

EXAM TRAPS (Common Mistakes)

  • Giving topical steroids — NEVER in epithelial HSV. This is the most dangerous mistake. Causes geographic ulcer.
  • Treating as bacterial without seeing dendrites — if contact lens wearer, check for dendritic pattern before assuming bacterial
  • Missing decreased corneal sensation — this clue strongly suggests HSV over bacterial
  • Treating as "viral conjunctivitis" — conjunctivitis has NO corneal ulcer, NO vision loss, NO photophobia
  • Confusing with herpes zoster ophthalmicus (HZO) — HZO has vesicular dermatomal rash (V1 distribution), Hutchinson sign (rash on tip of nose = high risk of ocular involvement), and may cause pseudodendrites (but coarser, less branching)

KEY NUMBERS & PATTERNS

  • Recurrence rate: 25% within 2 years, 50% within 10 years
  • Latency site: Trigeminal ganglion (CNV1)
  • Most common cause of corneal blindness in developed countries
  • Dendritic ulcer morphology: Branching pattern with terminal bulbs ("rose thorns")
  • Geographic ulcer size: >10mm (vs dendritic 2-3mm) — result of steroid use in epithelial HSV

Conjunctivitis (Bacterial vs Viral vs Allergic)

VIGNETTE PATTERN

Chief complaint: Red eye + discharge + irritation

Key negatives that rule out serious pathology:

  • Normal vision (if vision decreased, think keratitis or uveitis)
  • No pain (mild irritation OK, but deep pain suggests keratitis/uveitis/angle-closure)
  • No photophobia (photophobia = inflammation of iris/ciliary body)
  • No corneal involvement (fluorescein negative)

Memory aid: "Purulent = Bacterial. Preauricular node = Viral. Itching = Allergic."

SPECIAL CASES (High-Yield Variants)

1. HYPERACUTE BACTERIAL (Gonococcal)

  • Presentation: Copious purulent discharge ("pouring out"), severe chemosis, rapid onset (<12 hours)
  • Who: Neonates (vertical transmission) OR sexually active adults
  • Danger: Can perforate cornea within 24-48 hours if untreated
  • Treatment: Ceftriaxone 1g IM/IV (systemic, NOT just topical) + saline irrigation

2. CHLAMYDIAL CONJUNCTIVITIS (Inclusion conjunctivitis)

  • Presentation: Chronic mucopurulent discharge, follicles on tarsal conjunctiva
  • Who: Neonates (ophthalmia neonatorum, 5-14 days after birth) OR sexually active adults
  • Treatment: Oral azithromycin (1g single dose) OR doxycycline 100mg BID × 7 days. Treat partner.

3. EPIDEMIC KERATOCONJUNCTIVITIS (EKC)

  • Cause: Adenovirus (highly contagious)
  • Presentation: Watery discharge + preauricular node + subconjunctival hemorrhage + pseudomembranes
  • Complication: Subepithelial infiltrates (SEI) develop 7-14 days later → blurred vision for weeks/months
  • Management: Self-limited (2-4 weeks). Steroids ONLY if SEI causes vision loss. STRICT hand hygiene.

TREATMENT (By Type)

BACTERIAL:

  • Topical antibiotics: Erythromycin ointment OR fluoroquinolone drops (moxifloxacin, levofloxacin) QID × 5-7 days
  • Most resolve even without treatment (self-limited in 7-10 days), but antibiotics shorten course

VIRAL:

  • Supportive care only: Cool compresses, artificial tears, hand hygiene
  • Self-resolves in 2-4 weeks
  • No antibiotics needed (common exam trap — don't give antibiotics for viral)

ALLERGIC:

  • Mild: Antihistamine drops (olopatadine, ketotifen) OR mast cell stabilizers
  • Moderate-severe: Topical steroids (short course)
  • Avoid allergen, cool compresses

EXAM TRAPS

  • Giving antibiotics for viral conjunctivitis — self-limited, supportive care only
  • Missing gonococcal conjunctivitis — needs systemic ceftriaxone (topical alone insufficient), can perforate
  • Confusing with keratitis — keratitis has corneal involvement (fluorescein+), photophobia, vision loss
  • Not treating chlamydia systemically — needs oral azithromycin/doxycycline, not just topical
  • Missing preauricular lymph node — this finding = viral (adenovirus), NOT bacterial

KEY NUMBERS

  • Bacterial resolution: 7-10 days (with or without treatment)
  • Viral (adenovirus) resolution: 2-4 weeks
  • Gonococcal corneal perforation: can occur within 24-48 hours
  • Chlamydial onset in neonates: 5-14 days after birth (vs gonorrhea 2-5 days)
  • EKC subepithelial infiltrates: appear 7-14 days after onset, can last weeks to months

Central Retinal Artery vs Central Retinal Vein Occlusion

VIGNETTE PATTERN — CRAO (Arterial Occlusion)

Presentation: Sudden, PAINLESS, unilateral, PROFOUND vision loss ("like a curtain dropped”)

Vision severity: Count fingers or worse (hand motion, light perception, or no light perception)

Patient demographics: Elderly (>60 yo) with vascular risk factors

Risk factors:

  • Carotid artery disease (embolic source)
  • Atrial fibrillation (cardiac emboli)
  • Giant cell arteritis (GCA) — CRITICAL: if patient >50 yo + jaw claudication, headache, scalp tenderness, or polymyalgia symptoms
  • Hypercoagulable states, vasculitis

VIGNETTE PATTERN — CRVO (Venous Occlusion)

Presentation: Sudden, PAINLESS, unilateral vision loss (LESS severe than CRAO)

Vision severity: Blurred vision, can still read large print (20/100 to 20/400 range, rarely worse)

Patient demographics: Middle-aged to elderly (50-70 yo)

Risk factors:

  • Hypertension (most common)
  • Diabetes mellitus
  • Glaucoma (increased IOP compresses vein)
  • Hypercoagulable states (especially in young patients <50 yo)

Memory aid: "CRAO = Cherry-red + Complete blindness. CRVO = Hemorrhages everywhere (bloody thunder)."

IMMEDIATE MANAGEMENT

CRAO — Ocular Emergency (time = retina):

Goal: Restore perfusion within 90-100 minutes (retina tolerates ischemia for ~90 min before irreversible damage)

  • Ocular massage (firm pressure on closed eye for 5 sec, release 5 sec, repeat × 5 min) — may dislodge embolus
  • Lower IOP: Topical beta-blocker, acetazolamide 500mg IV, anterior chamber paracentesis (controversial)
  • Hyperbaric oxygen (if available, controversial)
  • Thrombolytics/intraarterial therapy (NOT standard, benefit unclear, high risk)

Workup for CRAO:

  • ESR + CRP — STAT if patient >50 yo (rule out GCA)
  • If GCA suspected: Start high-dose IV methylprednisolone IMMEDIATELY (do NOT wait for biopsy) — prevents vision loss in other eye
  • Carotid Doppler — evaluate for stenosis (source of emboli)
  • Echocardiogram — look for cardiac emboli (especially if Afib)
  • Hypercoagulable workup if young (<50 yo)

CRVO — Urgent (not emergent):

  • No acute intervention (damage already done)
  • Anti-VEGF injections (ranibizumab, bevacizumab) if macular edema develops
  • Monitor for neovascularization (ischemic CRVO → neovascular glaucoma risk in 2-3 months)

Workup for CRVO:

  • Blood pressure, fasting glucose, lipid panel
  • Hypercoagulable workup if young (<50 yo): protein C/S, factor V Leiden, antiphospholipid antibodies

EXAM TRAPS

  • Missing GCA in CRAO — if >50 yo, check ESR/CRP immediately. Start steroids BEFORE temporal artery biopsy if high suspicion.
  • Attempting thrombolytics routinely — NOT standard of care (high risk, unclear benefit, usually >24h window passed)
  • Confusing with retinal detachment — RD has floaters + curtain (gradual), NOT sudden total blackout. No cherry-red spot.
  • Assuming CRVO is benign — ischemic CRVO can lead to neovascular glaucoma ("90-day glaucoma"), needs close follow-up
  • Not checking for hypercoagulable state in young patients — CRVO in <50 yo warrants extensive workup

KEY NUMBERS

  • CRAO ischemic window: ~90-100 minutes for reversible damage
  • GCA association: 1-2% of CRAO cases (but devastating if missed)
  • CRVO neovascular glaucoma: develops in 2-3 months ("90-day glaucoma") in ischemic CRVO
  • CRVO vision prognosis: ~50% remain <20/200 (legal blindness) without treatment

Retinal Detachment

VIGNETTE PATTERN

Classic triad:

  • Flashes (photopsia) — sudden brief flashes of light, especially in peripheral vision
  • Floaters — dark spots, cobwebs, or "shower of dots" that don't move away when you blink
  • Curtain/shadow — dark curtain descending or ascending over visual field (progressive visual field defect)

Key features:

  • Painless (critical — pain suggests other pathology)
  • Peripheral vision loss first → progresses centrally
  • Symptoms develop over hours to days (NOT instantaneous like CRAO)

Risk factors:

  • High myopia (most common) — elongated globe → thinner retina, higher risk
  • Trauma (blunt or penetrating)
  • Post-cataract surgery (especially if complicated)
  • Age >50 (posterior vitreous detachment more common)
  • Family history of retinal detachment

RECOGNITION TRIGGER (Exam Findings)

Dilated fundoscopy (diagnostic):

  • Elevated, gray, wrinkled retina (looks "billowing" or "corrugated")
  • Loss of red reflex in area of detachment
  • Retinal tear/hole visible (U-shaped tear or horseshoe tear most common)

If media opacity (vitreous hemorrhage, cataract) blocks view: Use B-scan ultrasound — shows retina separated from choroid

Key: Curtain = RD. Diffuse blur = vitreous hemorrhage. Flashes + floaters WITHOUT curtain = likely benign PVD (but needs dilated exam to rule out tear).

IMMEDIATE MANAGEMENT

Confirmed retinal detachment:

  • Ophthalmology consult SAME DAY
  • Positioning: Patient should lie with detached side DOWN (e.g., if superior temporal detachment, lie on right side) — gravity helps reattach
  • No straining, bending, heavy lifting
  • NPO (surgery likely within 24-48 hours)

Surgical options (done by ophthalmology):

  • Pneumatic retinopexy (gas bubble injected) — for small, superior detachments
  • Scleral buckle (silicone band around eye) — compresses sclera to close tear
  • Vitrectomy (remove vitreous, flatten retina, laser/cryotherapy around tear)

Timing urgency:

  • Macula-ON detachment: Surgery within 24 hours (preserve central vision)
  • Macula-OFF detachment: Surgery same day but less emergent (central vision already lost, goal is prevent further detachment)

EXAM TRAPS

  • Reassuring patient with "just floaters" — flashes + floaters requires urgent dilated fundoscopy to rule out tear/detachment
  • Delaying referral — macula-on RD needs surgery within 24 hours to save central vision
  • Confusing with vitreous hemorrhage — both have floaters, but RD has curtain + localized field defect; VH has diffuse blur + red haze
  • Missing posterior vitreous detachment (PVD) as precursor — PVD can cause retinal tears in 10-15%, needs dilated exam even if asymptomatic
  • Not examining the fellow eye — if one eye has RD, other eye has 10-15% lifetime risk

KEY NUMBERS

  • Lifetime risk in general population: 0.5-1%
  • Risk in high myopes (>-6 D): 2-4%
  • Fellow eye risk after RD: 10-15%
  • Surgical success rate (anatomic reattachment): 85-90% after first surgery
  • Visual outcome if macula-on at surgery: 70-80% retain good vision (>20/40)
  • Visual outcome if macula-off >1 week: <50% achieve >20/40 (permanent central scotoma common)

Vitreous Hemorrhage

VIGNETTE PATTERN

Chief complaint: Sudden painless floaters + blurred vision

Floater description (pathognomonic):

  • "Cobwebs" or "spider webs"
  • "Shower of red rain" or "red haze"
  • "Dark curtain" (if massive)

Vision quality: Diffuse blurring (NOT localized curtain like retinal detachment)

Pain: PAINLESS (pain suggests globe rupture, endophthalmitis, or angle-closure)

Onset: Sudden (develops over minutes to hours)

RECOGNITION TRIGGER (Cause-Based Patterns)

Common causes — match vignette context to etiology:

Most common cause overall: Proliferative diabetic retinopathy (~30-40% of cases)

CONFIRMING FINDINGS

Physical exam:

  • Red reflex: Absent or decreased (blood blocks light)
  • Fundoscopy: Cannot visualize fundus (blood obscures view) OR see red/brown haze in vitreous
  • IOP: Normal (unless associated with other pathology)

Diagnostic test:

  • B-scan ultrasound — CRITICAL when fundus not visible. Shows vitreous opacities (blood) + RULES OUT retinal detachment
  • Once blood clears (weeks-months): dilated fundoscopy to identify cause (neovascularization, tears)

MANAGEMENT

Immediate workup:

  • B-scan ultrasound — MANDATORY to rule out retinal detachment (cannot see fundus, so must use ultrasound)
  • If retinal detachment found → urgent ophthalmology (same day)

Conservative management (if no RD):

  • Head elevation (sleep with head of bed elevated 30-45°) — allows blood to settle inferiorly, away from visual axis
  • Avoid Valsalva, heavy lifting, anticoagulants (if safe to hold)
  • Observation: Most clear spontaneously in 2-3 months

Interventions (once fundus visible or if no clearance):

  • Pan-retinal photocoagulation (PRP) — if proliferative diabetic retinopathy found (laser neovascularization)
  • Vitrectomy — if blood doesn't clear in 2-3 months OR if diabetic (earlier intervention to prevent complications)
  • Laser/cryotherapy to retinal tears — if tear identified

EXAM TRAPS

  • Discharging diabetic without ultrasound — MUST rule out retinal detachment with B-scan (cannot see fundus)
  • Confusing with retinal detachment — RD has curtain + flashes; VH has diffuse blur without curtain
  • Missing proliferative diabetic retinopathy as cause — needs pan-retinal photocoagulation once blood clears
  • Assuming all vitreous hemorrhage is benign — can be sign of retinal tear/detachment, needs urgent imaging

KEY NUMBERS

  • Spontaneous clearance: 2-3 months in most cases
  • Most common cause: Proliferative diabetic retinopathy (30-40%)
  • Retinal detachment association: 10-20% of vitreous hemorrhage cases have concurrent RD

Optic Neuritis

VIGNETTE PATTERN

Patient demographics: Young woman (20-40 years old) — peak age 30, female:male = 3:1

The pathognomonic triad:

  • Subacute vision loss — develops over hours to days (NOT sudden like CRAO, NOT gradual over months like cataract)
  • Pain with eye movement — PATHOGNOMONIC (90% of cases). Pain worsens when looking side-to-side or up/down.
  • Central scotoma — central or paracentral blind spot

Additional symptoms:

  • Dyschromatopsia — colors appear "washed out" or "desaturated" (especially red)
  • Uhthoff phenomenon — symptoms worsen with heat/exercise (classic for MS)
  • Vision severity: Ranges from mild blur (20/30) to severe loss (count fingers)

RECOGNITION TRIGGER (Exam Findings)

The diagnostic finding:

Afferent Pupillary Defect (APD / Marcus Gunn pupil)

  • Swinging flashlight test: When light swings to affected eye, BOTH pupils dilate (paradoxical dilation)
  • APD is present even if disc looks normal (retrobulbar neuritis)

Fundoscopy findings:

  • Retrobulbar neuritis (2/3 of cases): Disc appears NORMAL initially ("patient sees nothing, doctor sees nothing")
  • Anterior optic neuritis (1/3): Disc swelling (papillitis) — looks like papilledema but unilateral

Open-Angle Glaucoma (Primary Open-Angle Glaucoma)

VIGNETTE PATTERN

Presentation: Usually ASYMPTOMATIC until late (detected on screening exam)

When symptomatic (late disease):

  • Peripheral vision loss — "tunnel vision" (bumping into objects, difficulty driving)
  • Central vision preserved until very late (can still read 20/20 with end-stage glaucoma)

Patient demographics & risk factors:

  • Age: Elderly (>60 yo), risk increases with age
  • Race: African Americans >> Caucasians (3-4× higher risk, earlier onset, more severe)
  • Family history — first-degree relative with glaucoma
  • High myopia (elongated globe → thinner nerve fiber layer)
  • Diabetes, hypertension (controversial association)

RECOGNITION TRIGGER (Exam Findings)

The diagnostic triad:

  • Increased cup-to-disc ratio (C:D ratio >0.5 OR asymmetry >0.2 between eyes)
  • Optic nerve cupping — excavation of optic disc ("bean pot" appearance)
  • Peripheral visual field defects on automated perimetry (Humphrey visual field)

CRITICAL CONCEPT: IOP may be NORMAL

  • "Normal-tension glaucoma" — optic nerve damage with IOP <21 mmHg
  • Diagnosis based on optic nerve findings + visual field loss, NOT just IOP

Classic visual field patterns:

  • Nasal step — defect respecting horizontal midline
  • Arcuate scotoma — arc-shaped defect following nerve fiber layer
  • Tunnel vision — late-stage, only central island remains

DIAGNOSTIC TESTS

Essential tests for diagnosis:

  • Tonometry — measure IOP (normal 10-21 mmHg, but can be normal in glaucoma)
  • Gonioscopy — confirms angle is OPEN (differentiates from angle-closure)
  • Automated perimetry (Humphrey visual field) — detects peripheral field loss
  • OCT (optical coherence tomography) — quantifies retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thickness
  • Dilated fundoscopy — assess optic nerve head for cupping

TREATMENT (Goal: Lower IOP to Prevent Progression)

First-line medication:

  • Prostaglandin analogs (latanoprost, travoprost, bimatoprost) — FIRST-LINE
  • Mechanism: Increase uveoscleral outflow
  • Dosing: Once daily at bedtime
  • Side effects: Eyelash growth, periorbital pigmentation, iris darkening (permanent)

Second-line agents (if monotherapy insufficient):

  • Beta-blockers (timolol) — decrease aqueous production
  • Contraindications: Asthma, COPD, bradycardia, heart block
  • Alpha-agonists (brimonidine, apraclonidine) — decrease production + increase outflow
  • Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (dorzolamide, brinzolamide topical; acetazolamide oral) — decrease production
  • Rho kinase inhibitors (netarsudil) — increase outflow

Surgical options (if medications fail):

  • Laser trabeculoplasty (SLT, ALT) — first-line procedure, increases trabecular outflow
  • Trabeculectomy — creates new drainage pathway (filtration surgery)
  • Glaucoma drainage devices (shunts) — for refractory cases

  • Waiting for symptoms before diagnosing — by the time symptoms appear, irreversible damage has occurred

  • Confusing with cataract — cataract causes central blur, NOT peripheral vision loss

  • Prevalence in African Americans: 3-4× higher than Caucasians
  • Leading cause of irreversible blindness in African Americans
  • IOP reduction target: 20-30% from baseline (individualized)

TREATMENT (Stage-Specific)

Medical management (ALL stages):

  • Glycemic control — HbA1c <7% (DCCT trial: tight control reduces progression by 76%)
  • Blood pressure control — goal <140/90 (UKPDS: BP control reduces progression)
  • Lipid control — consider fenofibrate if dyslipidemia

Proliferative Diabetic Retinopathy (PDR):

  • Pan-retinal photocoagulation (PRP) — LASER to peripheral retina (1200-1600 burns)
  • Mechanism: Destroys ischemic retina → reduces VEGF → regresses neovascularization
  • Timing: Do BEFORE vitreous hemorrhage occurs

Diabetic Macular Edema (DME):

  • Anti-VEGF injections — FIRST-LINE (ranibizumab, bevacizumab, aflibercept)
  • Dosing: Monthly initially, then PRN based on OCT
  • Focal/grid laser — second-line if anti-VEGF contraindicated/ineffective
  • Intravitreal steroids — alternative (dexamethasone implant)

Vitrectomy: If non-clearing vitreous hemorrhage OR tractional retinal detachment

SCREENING GUIDELINES (High-Yield)

  • Type 1 diabetes: Start screening 5 years after diagnosis, then annually
  • Type 2 diabetes: Screen at diagnosis (may have had undiagnosed diabetes for years), then annually
  • Pregnancy: Pre-existing diabetics need exam in 1st trimester + close follow-up (retinopathy can progress rapidly)

EXAM TRAPS

  • Waiting for vision loss to refer — PDR needs laser BEFORE hemorrhage. Asymptomatic ≠ no disease.
  • Missing macular edema as cause of vision loss — treat with anti-VEGF, NOT just PRP
  • Confusing cotton-wool spots with hard exudates — cotton-wool = fluffy white (ischemia), hard exudates = yellow waxy (lipid)
  • Not screening Type 2 at diagnosis — 20% already have retinopathy at diagnosis
  • Using laser for macular edema as first-line — anti-VEGF is now first-line for DME

  • Prevalence: ~30% of diabetics have some retinopathy

  • Risk reduction with tight glycemic control: 76% (DCCT trial)
  • Type 2 at diagnosis: 20% already have retinopathy

Q44 : A 62-year-old hypertensive man with diabetes for 12 years wakes up with diplopia and a drooping right eyelid. Examination shows the right eye is 'down and out' but the pupil is normal and reactive. What is the most likely diagnosis and its expected course?
  • A) Posterior communicating artery aneurysm - urgent coiling
  • B) Microvascular (ischemic) third nerve palsy - pupil-sparing, resolves over about 3 months
  • C) Raised intracranial pressure - urgent lumbar puncture
  • D) Thyroid eye disease - orbital decompression
  • E) Cavernous sinus thrombosis - anticoagulation

Correct: B) Microvascular (ischemic) third nerve palsy - pupil-sparing, resolves over about 3 months

Concept: Pupil-sparing CN III palsy in a diabetic = microvascular Interpretation

Why B: A pupil-sparing third nerve palsy in an older patient with microvascular risk factors (hypertension, diabetes) is ischemic microvascular disease: the vasa nervorum infarcts, and because the pupillomotor fibers sit peripherally (most protected from central ischemia), the pupil is spared. It resolves spontaneously over about 3 months; the workup targets the risk factors.

Discriminator: Pupil-sparing + diabetic/ hypertensive = microvascular; the pupil-sparing pattern is what keeps the aneurysm off the table.

A) Posterior communicating artery aneurysm - urgent coilingAneurysm disease is pupil-INVOLVED - this palsy puts the pupil outside the danger zone
C) Raised intracranial pressure - urgent lumbar punctureRaised ICP causes bilateral VI palsies or papilledema - not an isolated pupil-sparing third nerve palsy
D) Thyroid eye disease - orbital decompressionThyroid eye disease limits elevation and retracts the lid - no fixed down-and-out pattern
E) Cavernous sinus thrombosis - anticoagulationCavernous sinus thrombosis is painful with proptosis, chemosis, and fever - not this picture

Trap: Pattern trap - the course (months, self-limited) and the spared pupil are inseparable; treating a pupil-sparing palsy as an aneurysm is the wrong emergency.

Future alert: Pupil-sparing CN III palsy in a diabetic/hypertensive = microvascular: check BP, HbA1c, lipids; observe; resolves ~3 months.

Q45 : A 45-year-old woman presents with diplopia that is horizontal and worse at distance. Examination shows an esotropia (inward-turning eye) with an inability to abduct the left eye, but no ptosis and no pupillary abnormality. Which cranial nerve is involved?
  • A) Left oculomotor nerve (III)
  • B) Left trochlear nerve (IV)
  • C) Left abducens nerve (VI)
  • D) Right oculomotor nerve (III)
  • E) Right trochlear nerve (IV)

Correct: C) Left abducens nerve (VI)

Concept: Inability to abduct = sixth nerve palsy Recall

Why C: The sixth (abducens) nerve innervates the lateral rectus; failure to abduct with an esotropia (crossed eye, worse at distance) is a sixth nerve palsy. Absent ptosis and a normal pupil rule out third nerve disease, and the abducting deficit is not trochlear (which tilts and fails depression in adduction).

Discriminator: Esotropia + failure to abduct = VI palsy; the abducting muscle is the lateral rectus and VI is its nerve.

A) Left oculomotor nerve (III)III nerve disease gives ptosis plus a down-and-out eye with possible pupil involvement - neither is present
B) Left trochlear nerve (IV)IV palsy causes hypertropia worse on down-gaze and head tilt - not an esotropia on abduction
D) Right oculomotor nerve (III)The deficit is in the LEFT eye abducting - the left VI is the culprit, not the right III
E) Right trochlear nerve (IV)Trochlear disease does not produce failure of abduction

Trap: Direction trap - map the action to the muscle to the nerve: abduction failure = lateral rectus = VI.

Future alert: Esotropia, worse at distance, no ptosis, failure to abduct = sixth nerve palsy; causes span microvascular, raised ICP, and cavernous sinus disease.

Q46 : A 40-year-old woman reports double vision that worsens toward the end of the day and when reading: her right ptosis becomes more obvious with sustained upgaze, and the diplopia varies during the examination. Pupils are normal. Which diagnosis fits best?
  • A) Third nerve palsy from an aneurysm
  • B) Myasthenia gravis
  • C) Sixth nerve palsy from raised ICP
  • D) Thyroid eye disease
  • E) Internuclear ophthalmoplegia from MS

Correct: B) Myasthenia gravis

Concept: Fatigable ptosis and variable diplopia = myasthenia Interpretation

Why B: Fatigability (worse at day's end), variability during the examination, and ptosis that worsens on sustained upgaze are the myasthenia signature - it fluctuates, unlike fixed cranial nerve palsies. The normal pupil is also characteristic: myasthenia never affects the pupil.

Discriminator: Variable + fatigable + pupil-normal = myasthenia; fixed deficits with a dilated pupil are the opposite category.

A) Third nerve palsy from an aneurysmAneurysmal third palsy is fixed, sudden, and pupil-dilated - none of that is here
C) Sixth nerve palsy from raised ICPRaised ICP gives a fixed abducens deficit, not fatigable variability
D) Thyroid eye diseaseThyroid eye disease is non-fatigable with lid retraction and proptosis
E) Internuclear ophthalmoplegia from MSINO gives a specific adduction failure on conjugate gaze testing - not fatigable fluctuating ptosis

Trap: Fluctuation trap - the exam plants temporal variation and fatigue; those belong to myasthenia alone among the diplopia causes.

Future alert: Fatigable ptosis + variable diplopia + normal pupils = myasthenia; edrophonium test and anti-AChR antibodies confirm.

Q47 : A 28-year-old obese woman presents with daily morning headaches for a month, blurred vision that darkens briefly for seconds when she stands up quickly, and ringing in her ears. Examination shows bilateral optic disc swelling with preserved visual acuity, no afferent pupillary defect, and normal neurological examination apart from this. What is the most likely diagnosis?
  • A) Bilateral optic neuritis
  • B) Papilledema from idiopathic intracranial hypertension (IIH)
  • C) Bilateral central retinal vein occlusion
  • D) Malignant hypertension
  • E) Optic nerve drusen

Correct: B) Papilledema from idiopathic intracranial hypertension (IIH)

Concept: IIH - classic demographic plus transient visual obscurations Interpretation

Why B: A young obese woman with morning headaches, pulsatile tinnitus, transient visual obscurations (seconds-long darkening, posture-related), and BILATERAL disc swelling with intact acuity = idiopathic intracranial hypertension. The bilateral swelling with preserved vision and no RAPD separates it from optic neuritis.

Discriminator: Bilateral disc swelling + young obese woman + TVOs + preserved acuity = IIH; the unilateral pain pattern would point to neuritis.

A) Bilateral optic neuritisOptic neuritis is typically unilateral with pain on eye movement, RAPD, and central scotoma - not this symmetric picture

Q48 : A 30-year-old woman is suspected of having papilledema. Which is the correct sequence of investigations, and which finding confirms the diagnosis of IIH?
  • A) Lumbar puncture first, then MRI if cerebrospinal fluid pressure is elevated
  • B) MRI and MR venography FIRST to exclude a mass or venous sinus thrombosis, then lumbar puncture; opening pressure above 25 cm H2O (with normal CSF) confirms IIH
  • C) CT angiography of the brain only
  • D) Visual fields first, and treatment only if fields are abnormal
  • E) No imaging needed - fundoscopy alone confirms papilledema

Correct: B) MRI and MR venography FIRST to exclude a mass or venous sinus thrombosis, then lumbar puncture; opening pressure above 25 cm H2O (with normal CSF) confirms IIH

Concept: Papilledema workup - imaging before LP; opening pressure threshold Analysis

Why B: Papilledema means raised intracranial pressure, so imaging (MRI + MRV) must come BEFORE lumbar puncture to exclude a mass or venous sinus thrombosis - doing an LP first in a patient with a mass risks herniation. IIH is confirmed by an opening pressure above 25 cm H2O with normal CSF content.

Discriminator: Imaging before LP is a safety mandate; the opening-pressure number (25 cm H2O) with normal CSF is the diagnostic lock.

A) Lumbar puncture first, then MRI if cerebrospinal fluid pressure is elevatedLP first is dangerous - herniation risk if a mass is present
C) CT angiography of the brain onlyCT angiography is for vascular lesions and does not rule out the mass/venous thrombosis spectrum or measure pressure
D) Visual fields first, and treatment only if fields are abnormalFields tell severity, not the diagnosis; they come after imaging and LP
E) No imaging needed - fundoscopy alone confirms papilledemaFundoscopy shows swelling but cannot prove ICP is raised - measured pressure is the diagnosis

Trap: Sequence trap - the exam tests whether you know LP comes AFTER imaging in any papilledema workup.

Future alert: Papilledema: MRI + MRV first (exclude mass/venous sinus thrombosis), then LP; opening pressure >25 cm H2O with normal CSF = IIH.

Q49 : A 32-year-old obese woman is diagnosed with IIH and mild visual field constriction. What is the FIRST-LINE treatment approach?
  • A) Immediate optic nerve sheath fenestration
  • B) Weight loss of 5-10% plus acetazolamide, escalating to surgery if vision deteriorates
  • C) Chronic oral steroids
  • D) Lumbar drain for one week
  • E) Neurosurgical shunt on diagnosis

Correct: B) Weight loss of 5-10% plus acetazolamide, escalating to surgery if vision deteriorates

Concept: IIH treatment pyramid - weight loss and acetazolamide first Recall

Why B: Treatment is graduated: weight loss of 5-10% is the most effective disease-modifying step, plus acetazolamide (500 mg to 1 g twice daily) which reduces CSF production. Serial LP, optic nerve sheath fenestration, or shunting are reserved for vision-threatening or refractory disease.

Discriminator: Start with weight loss + acetazolamide; surgery is for vision loss, not first-line.

A) Immediate optic nerve sheath fenestrationSurgery (fenestration) is a vision-salvage escalation, not the opening move
C) Chronic oral steroidsSteroids are not IIH therapy - they do not lower CSF pressure and have their own risks
D) Lumbar drain for one weekA lumbar drain is temporary and invasive, not a treatment plan
E) Neurosurgical shunt on diagnosisShunting at diagnosis over-treats; most IIH responds to weight loss and acetazolamide

Trap: Staging trap - the exam escalates treatment too fast; the pyramid (lifestyle and drug first) is the graded answer.

Future alert: IIH: weight loss 5-10% + acetazolamide first-line; add serial LP; fenestration or shunt only if vision deteriorates.

Correct: B) Pseudopapilledema from optic disc drusen - no true swelling, autofluorescence or B-scan can confirm buried drusen

Concept: Pseudopapilledema - disc drusen mimic swelling Analysis

Why B: Bilateral disc elevation in an asymptomatic patient is the classic pseudopapilledema scenario: buried optic disc drusen create a lumpy, elevated disc that mimics swelling without raised ICP. Autofluorescence (drusen fluoresce) or B-scan ultrasound can confirm buried drusen - the key is not to treat a normal-pressure disc as papilledema.
